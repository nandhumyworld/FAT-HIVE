⚡ Where Claude Can Fail — Confidence vs Accuracy

Claude can sound confident while being wrong. Understanding where it fails is the first step to using it safely.

⚠️
The Core Mismatch

Claude's confidence in its response is not a reliable indicator of its accuracy. It can express high certainty about incorrect facts, made-up citations, and outdated information.

Known Failure Zones

Failure TypeWhat HappensRisk Level
HallucinationPlausible but fabricated facts, names, or citationsHIGH
Knowledge CutoffOutdated information presented as currentHIGH
Confidently Wrong NumbersStatistics, percentages, dates stated with false precisionHIGH
SycophancyAgreeing with user assumptions rather than correcting themMEDIUM
OversimplificationNuanced topics flattened into neat answersMEDIUM
Context DriftLosing important constraints in long conversationsMEDIUM
Prompt Following vs ThinkingDoing exactly what was asked even if it's the wrong thingLOW–MED
❌ High-Risk Scenarios
  • Citing a specific study or statistic
  • Naming a law, regulation, or policy
  • Describing a real person's statement or position
  • Providing recent market data or news
  • Giving medical, legal, or financial specifics
✅ Lower-Risk Scenarios
  • Generating structure, frameworks, and templates
  • Brainstorming ideas (verify before committing)
  • Explaining well-established concepts
  • Reformatting or summarising your own content
  • Drafting first versions for human review

🌀 Hallucination Awareness — Why It Happens & How to Spot It

Hallucination is not a bug Claude will eventually fix — it is a fundamental characteristic of how large language models work. Understanding why helps you predict when it will occur.

Claude predicts the most plausible next token based on patterns in its training data — it does not "look things up" in a database of facts. When asked for something outside its reliable knowledge, it generates plausible-sounding text that pattern-matches to what an answer should look like.

🔬 Conditions That Increase Hallucination Risk
1
Specific, verifiable facts

Exact figures, dates, names, citations — things that have a correct answer Claude must retrieve, not generate

2
Obscure or niche domains

Less training data = more "filling in" from pattern matching, increasing fabrication

3
Events after knowledge cutoff

Claude may extrapolate or fabricate recent developments it was never trained on

4
Pressure to be helpful

If you expect a specific answer, Claude may produce one — even if it shouldn't

These signals don't confirm hallucination — but they should trigger your verification instinct:

📍Very specific statistics, percentages, or exact datesVERIFY
📚Named studies, reports, papers, or publicationsVERIFY
👤Attributed quotes from real peopleVERIFY
⚖️Specific laws, clauses, or regulatory detailsVERIFY
🗓️Recent events or announcements (post-training data)DO NOT RELY
🧠Claude says "I'm not sure" — but then answers anywayVERIFY
📝Creative structure and frameworks (not citing facts)LOWER RISK

Use these prompts to surface uncertainty before trusting a response:

Confidence Check
Before I use this — how confident are you in [specific claim]?
What's the source or basis for that figure?
If you're uncertain, tell me what you don't know rather than guessing.
Verification Request
For each specific fact, statistic, or citation in your response:
1. Flag it with [VERIFY] if you're not certain of its accuracy
2. Tell me what search terms or sources I should use to confirm it
3. Restate anything you're confident in without the [VERIFY] flag
🕵️ Exercise 1 — Hallucination Hunt

Deliberately Surface and Catch a Hallucination

  • Ask Claude: "What are 3 published studies on remote work productivity from 2022–2024? Cite the authors and journals."
  • Then ask: "How confident are you in each citation? Mark any you're uncertain of with [UNVERIFIED]."
  • Search for one of the cited papers. Did it exist exactly as described?
  • Discuss: What changed in Claude's tone when pressed for confidence?

✅ Fact Verification Habits — When to Trust vs Verify

The goal isn't to verify everything — that would eliminate all productivity gains. The goal is to verify the right things at the right times.

🔍 The Trust Spectrum — Know Where You Are
✓
Trust and Use (with awareness)

Structure, frameworks, brainstormed ideas, rewrites of your own content, explanations of well-known concepts. Still do a logic/sense check.

!
Spot-Check Recommended

Technical procedures, code logic, process descriptions, industry practices. Verify the key steps that matter most.

✗
Always Independently Verify

Statistics, citations, legal/regulatory specifics, medical details, financial figures, recent events. Never publish or act on these without primary source confirmation.

The 3-Question Verification Decision

🤔
Before using any AI-generated claim, ask yourself:

1. Stakes: What happens if this is wrong? Low stakes = lower verification bar. High stakes = verify always.
2. Specificity: Is this a specific, verifiable fact — or a framework/structure? Facts need verification; structure needs a sense check.
3. Recency: Does this depend on current information? If yes, Claude's training cutoff makes it unreliable without a live source.

Verification Triage Prompt
Review your response above and categorise each claim as:
[SAFE] — well-established concept, no single correct answer, or logic-based
[CHECK] — specific enough that it could be wrong; I should sense-check it  
[VERIFY] — specific fact, statistic, citation, or recent event — I must verify from a primary source

For each [VERIFY] item, give me the best search query to confirm it.
✅ Exercise 2 — Triage a Claude Response

Apply the Trust Spectrum to a Real Output

  • Ask Claude to write a paragraph on a topic in your field (market trends, a technical concept, a regulatory area)
  • Run the Verification Triage Prompt on the output
  • For each [VERIFY] item — search for the primary source. Was Claude right?
  • Reflect: Did Claude correctly identify its own uncertain claims?

🔒 Safe Handling of Sensitive & Confidential Content

Claude processes everything you share in the context of your conversation. Knowing what to share — and what to withhold — protects your organisation, your colleagues, and your clients.

❌ Never Share with Claude
  • Customer or client personal data (PII)
  • Passwords, API keys, credentials
  • Unreleased financial results or forecasts
  • M&A plans or commercially sensitive strategy
  • Patient health information (HIPAA-protected)
  • Attorney-client privileged communications
  • Employee personal or HR records
✅ Safe to Share (Generally)
  • Anonymised or fictional data for testing
  • Publicly available information
  • Your own ideas and drafts
  • General business context (non-sensitive)
  • Code that doesn't embed credentials
  • Documents already published externally
  • Generic role descriptions and job titles
⚠️
The Anonymisation Technique

Replace real names, companies, and identifying details with generic placeholders before sharing context with Claude. "Company X is planning to acquire Company Y" → "Acme Corp is planning to acquire Beta Ltd" tells Claude everything it needs without revealing the real parties.

Sensitive Content Red Flags to Watch For

📧Pasting an email chain with client names and project detailsANONYMISE FIRST
📊Sharing a spreadsheet with real customer recordsDO NOT SHARE
💻Pasting code that contains hardcoded API keys or passwordsREMOVE FIRST
📋Asking Claude to review an HR complaint with real employee namesANONYMISE FIRST
📝Sharing a contract template (without client details)GENERALLY SAFE
🗂️Describing a business challenge in general termsGENERALLY SAFE

👁️ Human Review Practices — Building Review Checkpoints

AI can accelerate your work by a factor of 3–10x. But speed without review creates risk. The professionals who use AI best are those who design the review step — not those who skip it.

📋 The AI Workflow Review Model
1
Generate

Use Claude to produce a first draft, analysis, or structured output. Move fast here — this is where AI saves time.

2
Scan for Risk Signals

Specifically check: specific statistics, citations, legal/regulatory claims, recent events. These are highest-risk for hallucination.

3
Verify the Critical Items

Search primary sources for anything in your output that will be published, shared externally, or acted upon.

4
Sense-Check the Logic

Does the structure, argument, or recommendation actually make sense for your context? AI can produce logically coherent but contextually wrong output.

5
Personalise and Own It

Add your expertise, context, and voice. The final output should reflect your professional judgment — not just AI output with your name on it.

💡
The "Whose Name Is On It?" Rule

Before using any AI-generated output externally, ask: if this turned out to be wrong, whose credibility is at risk? If it's yours — the review standard needs to match what you'd apply to your own work.

💻 Claude Code Safety — Reviewing Agentic File Changes

Claude Code can autonomously read, write, and modify files across your codebase. This is powerful — and requires a specific review mindset that differs from prompt-response interactions.

🚨
Agentic Actions Are Often Irreversible

When Claude Code deletes a file, overwrites a configuration, or refactors across 30 files — it has already happened. Unlike a response you can ignore, agentic file changes are real. Git history, backups, and careful scoping are your safety net.

Before You Start — Scope and Protect

❌ Risky Starting Positions
  • No version control / uncommitted changes
  • Running in production environment
  • Vague instructions: "Fix the codebase"
  • No review of what Claude plans to do first
  • Granting write access to unrelated directories
✅ Safe Starting Positions
  • Clean Git commit before every Claude Code session
  • Work in dev / staging — not production
  • Scope clearly: "Only modify files in /src/auth"
  • Ask Claude to summarise its plan before executing
  • Review diffs before accepting bulk changes

The Pre-Execution Prompt Habit

Safe Claude Code Start
Before you make any changes:
1. Tell me which files you plan to modify
2. Summarise what each change will do and why
3. Flag any files you plan to DELETE
4. Wait for my "go ahead" before executing

Scope constraint: Only work within [specific directory or feature area].
Do not modify [list any protected files or directories].

Reading the Diff — What to Look For

// auth/service.ts — reviewing Claude Code changes
- const SESSION_TIMEOUT = 3600; // 1 hour
+ const SESSION_TIMEOUT = 86400; // 24 hours ← Did you intend this? Security implication.
- if (user.role === 'admin') { validateMFA(user); }
+ // MFA check removed for performance ← STOP. This is a security regression.
+ // TODO: update tests ← Claude often defers tests. Review immediately.
🔍
Specific Things to Check in Every Diff

Security-related changes (auth, sessions, permissions) · Configuration changes in production files · Deleted or renamed files · Dependencies added to package.json · Comments that say "TODO" or "temporary" · Changes to more files than requested

💻 Exercise 3 — Safe Claude Code Session

Practice the Pre-Execution Review Habit

  • Open Claude Code on a test or personal project (not production)
  • Commit all current changes first: git commit -am "checkpoint before AI session"
  • Give a scoped task: "Refactor the login function in auth.py — summarise your plan first, then wait."
  • Review the plan. Ask: does this match what you intended?
  • Accept the changes, then run git diff HEAD~1 to review what actually changed

🔌 MCP Safety — Permissions, Data Exposure & Scoping

MCP (Model Context Protocol) lets Claude connect to real external tools — calendars, CRMs, databases, file systems, email. This unlocks powerful automation but requires deliberate permission management.

🔌
MCP Means Real Actions on Real Systems

When Claude uses an MCP server to send an email, create a database record, or delete a file — that action is real and immediate. Unlike a suggestion you can ignore, MCP-triggered actions require your explicit intent and awareness before they execute.

Apply the principle of least privilege — grant only what's needed for the specific task.

✅Grant read-only access when write access isn't neededDO THIS
✅Review what permissions each MCP server requests before connectingDO THIS
✅Revoke MCP access for tools you're no longer actively usingDO THIS
⚠️Connecting MCP tools with broad access "just in case"AVOID
⚠️Leaving admin-level MCP access enabled in casual conversationsAVOID
⚠️Assuming Claude won't use a tool you haven't explicitly asked it to useCHECK FIRST

When Claude accesses your tools via MCP, the data in those tools flows into its context window. Understand what it can see:

MCP ToolData Claude Can AccessRisk to Consider
Google CalendarEvent titles, attendees, times, descriptionsAttendee names, meeting subjects exposed
GmailEmail content, senders, recipients, attachmentsConfidential communications in context
Google DriveFile contents, metadata, sharing settingsSensitive documents loaded into context
CRM (e.g. Salesforce)Customer data, deals, communicationsClient PII and commercial data exposure
Database toolsQuery results, table structuresPII, financial, or production data leakage

Scope MCP access to the minimum needed for each session. Use explicit constraints in your prompts:

Scoped MCP Task
Using the [Tool Name] connection:
- Only access data from [specific folder / project / date range]
- Do not access or modify [specify what's off-limits]
- Before taking any write action, tell me what you plan to do and wait for confirmation
- If you're uncertain whether an action is in scope, ask me first
💡
Prompt-Level Guardrail

Include "Before taking any write action, tell me and wait for my confirmation" in every MCP-enabled session. This single instruction prevents most unintended actions.

🔌 Exercise 4 — MCP Permission Audit

Review Your Connected MCP Tools

  • In Claude.ai settings, find your connected MCP apps/tools
  • For each connected tool: What write permissions does it have? Is that the minimum needed?
  • Identify any tools connected that you haven't used recently — consider revoking
  • Draft a "scoped MCP session" prompt for a tool you use regularly

🖥️ Cowork Safety — Desktop Automation Scope Management

Cowork is Claude's desktop automation agent — it can control your computer, move files, interact with applications, and execute sequences of actions autonomously. This power demands careful scoping.

🖥️
Desktop Automation = Physical Access to Your Machine

Cowork can interact with any application visible on your screen, move or delete files, and execute multi-step workflows. A vague or overly broad instruction can trigger a cascade of unintended actions across your system.

Scope Limiting Rules for Cowork

🖥️ Cowork Safety Checklist
1
State exactly which folders or applications are in scope

"Only operate within the /Projects/ClientABC folder" prevents Cowork from wandering across your file system.

2
Explicitly list what Cowork must not touch

"Do not access my Downloads folder, email client, or any application not mentioned" is a valid and important instruction.

3
Use "pause before delete" as a standing rule

Always include: "If any step involves deleting or moving files permanently, stop and confirm with me first."

4
Watch the screen during long automation runs

Cowork benefits from human presence. Don't walk away during complex multi-step sequences — be ready to intervene.

5
Never run Cowork with elevated permissions by default

Avoid running desktop automation as an administrator unless the specific task requires it. Limit blast radius.

Safe Cowork Session Start
Task: [Specific automation task]

Scope constraints:
- Only operate within: [folder path or application name]
- Do not access: [list off-limits areas]
- Before any file deletion, move, or rename: stop and confirm with me
- If you encounter an unexpected state or error: stop and report rather than improvising

Confirm you understand these constraints before starting.

🔐 Data Privacy — What NOT to Share in Professional Settings

Data privacy in AI usage isn't just an IT concern — it's a professional responsibility. Sharing the wrong data with Claude can expose your organisation to regulatory, legal, and reputational risk.

👤
Personal Data (PII)

Names, addresses, emails, phone numbers, ID numbers — any information that identifies a real individual

🏥
Health Information

Medical records, diagnoses, treatment data — protected under HIPAA and equivalent regulations globally

💳
Financial Data

Account numbers, card details, transaction records — both personal and corporate

⚖️
Legal Privileged Content

Attorney-client communications, case strategy, litigation documents

🔑
Credentials & Secrets

Passwords, API keys, tokens, private keys — never share these in any context

📈
Inside Information

Unreleased financials, M&A activity, material non-public information (MNPI)

You can share the structure of sensitive content while protecting the actual data — this gives Claude enough context to help without exposing real information:

❌ "Help me respond to Sarah Chen's complaint about Invoice #INV-2024-8823"
✅ "Help me respond to a client complaint about a billing error on a recent invoice"
❌ Pasting a spreadsheet with real customer names and order values
✅ Describing the structure: "I have columns: CustomerID, OrderDate, Amount, Status"
❌ "Our Q3 revenue was £4.2M, 23% below target — help me explain this to the board"
✅ "Our Q3 revenue was significantly below target — help me structure a board explanation"

Different regulatory regimes apply depending on your sector and geography. Know which applies to you:

🏢 Organisational AI Policies — Compliance & Company Guidelines

Most organisations are still developing their AI usage policies. Whether formal or informal, knowing how to navigate and contribute to these policies is now a professional skill.

⚠️ If No Policy Exists Yet
  • Apply your data handling standards as if AI were any other third-party tool
  • Default to the most restrictive interpretation for sensitive data
  • Document your AI usage so it can be reviewed later
  • Raise the policy gap with your manager or IT team
  • Use this module as your baseline framework
✅ If a Policy Exists
  • Know specifically which tools are approved for professional use
  • Understand data classification tiers (public, internal, confidential)
  • Know what requires approval before new AI tool adoption
  • Understand disclosure requirements for AI-assisted work
  • Know who to contact with questions or incidents

Questions to Ask Your Organisation

Which AI tools are approved for professional use? ▼

Organisations typically maintain an approved tool list. Using unapproved AI tools — even well-known ones — may violate IT security policy and create data handling risks that the organisation hasn't assessed. When in doubt, ask IT or your manager before introducing a new AI tool to your workflow.

What data classification framework applies? ▼

Most organisations classify data into tiers — typically Public, Internal, Confidential, and Restricted. AI tools should only be used with data at the classification level that's been assessed and approved. Confidential and Restricted data typically require either a specific enterprise agreement with the AI provider or are prohibited from AI tool use entirely.

Is AI-assisted work subject to disclosure requirements? ▼

Some organisations (and some clients) require disclosure when AI has been used to produce deliverables. This is particularly common in legal, financial, consulting, and creative sectors. Know whether your organisation has a disclosure policy — and whether your clients have their own requirements that your organisation must honour.

Who owns AI-generated output? ▼

IP ownership of AI-generated content is an evolving legal area. Most organisations take the position that output produced using their systems and for their purposes is the company's IP. However, this may be more complex where individual employees use personal accounts or tools. Check whether your organisation has taken a position on this.

What's the incident reporting process for AI-related issues? ▼

If you accidentally share sensitive data with an AI tool, or if you suspect AI-generated content caused a problem — know who to contact. Most organisations with AI policies have an incident escalation path. If none exists, your data protection officer, IT security team, or line manager is the right starting point.

The Responsible AI Mindset in Practice

🎯
You Are the Professional — Not Claude

When you put your name or your organisation's name on any output, you're taking responsibility for it. Use Claude as a powerful tool — but always exercise your professional judgment before committing to any AI-generated content. The AI accelerates your thinking; it doesn't replace it.

This is especially important for:

  • → Client-facing documents and communications
  • → Technical decisions with downstream consequences
  • → Analysis that drives business decisions
  • → Code committed to production systems
🔍
Fluency Isn't the Same as Accuracy

Claude writes in a clear, confident, well-structured style. This fluency can create an illusion of authority. Develop a habit of mentally separating "how well-written is this?" from "how accurate is this?" — these are completely different questions.

When Claude sounds authoritative, ask yourself:

  • → Am I reading this as credible because it's accurate — or because it's well-written?
  • → Does this align with what I know from direct experience?
  • → Have I asked Claude where this claim comes from?
  • → Would I act on this without an independent check?
🌱
Earn Trust Through Experience

The right approach to AI trust is empirical — verify enough outputs in a domain to calibrate how reliable Claude is in that context. Once you've seen it hallucinate citations, you'll always check citations. Once you've seen it get technical details right repeatedly, you'll extend more trust (with continued awareness).

A calibrated trust model:

Domain / TaskSuggested Starting Trust Level
Brainstorming and ideationHigh — ideas need evaluation regardless of source
Drafting and structureHigh — you'll edit anyway; the structure is the value
Technical explanations (general)Medium — verify before teaching others
Code (logic and approach)Medium — test before committing
Specific facts and citationsLow — always verify from primary sources
Legal, medical, financial specificsVery Low — always consult relevant professionals
